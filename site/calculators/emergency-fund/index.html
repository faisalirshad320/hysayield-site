Where the fund will sit.

An emergency fund should cover 3 to 6 months of essential expenses — the costs you'd still have to pay if your income stopped — and more if your income is irregular or you support others. It isn't a percentage of salary; it's your essential monthly spending times the months of cover you need.

On essential costs of $3,200/month, six months is $19,200. From $4,000 saved and $400 a month at 4% APY, you'd reach it in 35 months — and the finished fund would earn about $768 a year.

How many months of cover you need

MonthsUsually right when…
3Two steady incomes, no dependents, skills that are quick to re-hire
6One income, or dependents, or a mortgage — the most common recommendation
9–12Self-employed, commission-based, seasonal work, a single income supporting a family, or a specialised job that takes longer to replace
12+Near retirement, chronic health costs, or a small business owner whose household and business cash overlap

What counts as an essential expense

Only what you would still pay if your income stopped tomorrow: housing, food, utilities, transport to look for work, insurance, minimum debt payments and genuine necessities. Leave out subscriptions, eating out, travel and extra debt payments — you'd cut those first. Using your full spending overstates the target and makes it feel unreachable.

Where to keep it

  • A high-yield savings account is the standard answer: FDIC-insured, available within a day or two, and earning interest. At the FDIC national average savings rate of 0.37% (September 21, 2026), a $19,200 fund earns about $71 a year; at 4% it earns $768.
  • Not a CD, unless it's a small slice in a ladder — the early-withdrawal penalty defeats the purpose.
  • Not a brokerage account. Emergencies and market falls tend to arrive together, and you'd be forced to sell low.
  • A HELOC is a backup, not a fund. Banks can freeze or cut home-equity lines in a downturn — exactly when you'd reach for one — and it's borrowing, not savings.

Building it

  1. Aim first for one month of essentials — it covers most car repairs and medical bills.
  2. Automate a transfer on payday into a separate savings account, so it isn't mixed with spending money.
  3. Send windfalls — tax refunds, bonuses — straight to it until you reach the target.
  4. Refill it after you use it before going back to other goals.

Rental property owners

Keep a separate reserve per property, typically 3–6 months of its costs (mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance), plus a capital-expenditure fund for roofs, boilers and appliances. Vacancies and repairs don't wait for your personal fund to recover.

Questions

How much should I have in my emergency fund?

Three to six months of essential expenses for most people; nine to twelve if your income is irregular, you're self-employed, or you're the only earner supporting a family.

Is $10,000 too much for an emergency fund?

Only if it's more than about six to twelve months of your essential costs. For someone spending $3,000 a month on essentials, $10,000 is just over three months — reasonable, not excessive.

Is $50,000 too much for an emergency fund?

It depends on your costs. At $4,000 a month in essentials it's about a year — sensible if your income is irregular, more than most salaried people need. Money above your target could go to higher-returning goals.

Where should I keep my emergency fund?

In a separate, FDIC-insured high-yield savings account: safe, reachable in a day or two, and earning interest. Not a CD (penalty) and not investments (market risk).

Can a HELOC be my emergency fund?

It can be a backup, but not the fund itself. Lenders can freeze or reduce home-equity lines during downturns, and it's debt you'd have to repay with interest.
