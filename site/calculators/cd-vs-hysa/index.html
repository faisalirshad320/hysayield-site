CD vs high-yield savings

The trade is rate certainty against access. Here is what each side is actually worth.

Negative if you expect rates to fall.
Months of interest, as your bank quotes it.

A CD locks your rate and your money. A high-yield savings account keeps both flexible. Which wins comes down to two things: whether savings rates fall during the term, and whether you might need the cash before it ends.

On $25,000 over 2 years, a 4.70% CD returns $2,405 against $2,301 from a savings account holding 4.50% — a difference of $105. Break that CD early with a 6-month interest penalty and you give up $575, which is more than the advantage. Put your own numbers in below.

What the break-even rate tells you

The calculator gives a break-even APY: the flat savings rate that would have matched the CD over the same term. If you think savings rates will average above it, the savings account wins; below it, the CD does. It turns a guess about the future into one number you can actually have an opinion about.

When each one makes sense

A CD is the better fit when

  • You are confident you will not need the money before the term ends.
  • Rates look more likely to fall than rise, and you want to lock today's.
  • The money has a date attached — a deposit, a tax bill, a planned purchase — that matches the term.

A high-yield savings account is the better fit when

  • It is your emergency fund. Access is the whole point, and a penalty defeats it.
  • The CD premium is small. A 0.20% gap on $25,000 over two years is about $100 — thin payment for locking up the money.
  • You would be tempted to break the CD. The penalty can exceed the interest earned so far, leaving you with less than you deposited.

A note on CD ladders

Splitting the money across CDs maturing at different dates gives you part of the rate lock and part of the access. It is a reasonable middle path, and it is also more admin than most people sustain. If you would not actually roll each rung on time, the simpler account usually wins in practice.
