What most savings calculators get wrong

A 5% APY must return exactly 5% over a year — whatever the compounding frequency. Several calculators that rank for this search treat APY as a plain interest rate and compound it again, overstating the result.

APY already includes compounding. That is the whole point of the number: it is the figure US banks are required to quote so that accounts can be compared like for like. Feeding an APY into a compound-interest formula as though it were a nominal rate double-counts, and the error grows with the balance and the term.

This calculator derives the periodic rate from the APY, so a 5% APY returns 5.00% in a year on daily, monthly, quarterly or annual compounding. How to calculate interest on a savings account

Three cases, from simplest to the one this calculator exists for:

  1. One year, no deposits: interest = balance × APY. $10,000 × 4.50% = $450.00.
  2. Any period, no deposits: interest = balance × ((1 + APY)months ÷ 12 − 1). Six months at 4.50% on $10,000 is $222.52 — a little less than half a year's interest, because compounding is back-loaded.
  3. With monthly deposits: each deposit earns from the month it lands, so the balance is a sum of many little compounding streams. That is what the schedule above works out month by month. The APY formula page shows the algebra; simple interest is the version without compounding.

The terms the calculator uses

  • APY — annual percentage yield, the return over a year including compounding; the number US banks must quote under Regulation DD. What APY means.
  • Compounding frequency — how often interest is credited. Once APY is fixed, it changes almost nothing; here is why.
  • Marginal tax rate — the federal and state brackets your next dollar of income falls in; savings interest is taxed at those rates and reported on Form 1099-INT. How the tax works.
  • Real value — the balance divided by cumulative inflation, i.e. what it buys in today's dollars.
  • Ordinary annuity — deposits assumed at the end of each month, the conservative convention. All terms are in the glossary.

Questions

How do I calculate interest on a savings account?

Multiply the balance by the APY for a full year: $10,000 × 4.50% = $450. For part of a year, use balance × ((1 + APY)months ÷ 12 − 1). With regular deposits it gets fiddly, which is what the calculator above is for. For simple (non-compounding) interest, use the simple interest calculator.

How much does $10,000 earn in a high-yield savings account?

At 4.50% APY, $10,000 left alone earns $450 in the first year and grows to $12,462 after five years. Change the APY above to match your bank — and see the full table for $10,000 at other rates.

Does compounding daily beat compounding monthly?

Barely. Once a bank quotes APY, the compounding frequency is already inside that number, so two accounts at the same APY pay the same regardless of how often they compound. Frequency only matters when a bank quotes a plain interest rate instead.

Do I pay tax on high-yield savings interest?

In the US, yes — savings interest is taxed as ordinary income in the year it is credited, and your bank issues a Form 1099-INT once you earn $10 or more. Enter your brackets under "Tax and inflation" to see the after-tax figure. More on the tax treatment.

Is a CD better than a high-yield savings account?

A CD locks your rate and your money; a HYSA keeps both flexible. Which wins depends on where rates go and whether you might need the cash early. The CD vs HYSA calculator puts numbers on it, including the early-withdrawal penalty.

Is my money safe?

A high-yield savings account at an FDIC-insured US bank is covered to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, exactly like a branch account. What is not guaranteed is the rate: every HYSA rate is variable and can drop at any time.
