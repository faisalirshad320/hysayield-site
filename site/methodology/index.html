How the calculators work

Every formula, every assumption, and the tests the engine has to pass before it ships.

All calculations start from the APY your bank quotes and derive the periodic rate from it, so that a given APY returns exactly that APY over a year at any compounding frequency. Monthly deposits are added at the end of each month. Tax is applied to interest only. Everything runs in your browser.

The shipped JavaScript engine is checked on every build against an independently written Python implementation: 3,840 comparisons over 960 input combinations agree to within 2e-13 relative error, and every property test passes.

The core formula

From the APY, the rate for each compounding period is:

r = (1 + APY)1/n − 1

where n is 365 for daily, 12 for monthly, 4 for quarterly and 1 for annual compounding. Each month, the balance is compounded by however many whole periods fall inside that month — 30 or 31 for daily compounding, carried forward so a year always contains exactly 365 — and then the month's deposit is added.

Why start from APY

US banks must quote APY on deposit accounts under the Truth in Savings Act and Regulation DD. APY is defined as the return over a year including compounding. A calculator that feeds an APY into a compound-interest formula as if it were a nominal rate compounds it a second time and overstates the result. Starting from APY avoids that by construction.

Assumptions

  • Deposits are made at the end of each month (an ordinary annuity), which matches a standing transfer.
  • The rate is held flat for the whole term, except in the CD comparison, where you can set a yearly drift.
  • Tax = interest × (federal rate + state rate), capped at 95%. It is a simple marginal-rate estimate; it ignores deductions, credits and brackets changing over time.
  • Real value = final balance ÷ (1 + inflation)years.
  • CD early-withdrawal penalty = months of penalty × one month's interest on the principal at the CD's APY.
  • Reverse solver: balance = monthly target ÷ ((1 + APY)1/12 − 1), grossed up by 1 ÷ (1 − tax rate) for after-tax targets.

How it is tested

  1. Hand-checked cases. Pure compound interest, a zero-rate annuity, a closed-form annuity, tax on interest only, the reverse solver, inflation, and a CD tie at equal rates.
  2. APY invariance. 5.00% APY must return exactly $10,500 on $10,000 over one year at every compounding frequency.
  3. Independent cross-check. The shipped JavaScript iterates month by month. A separate Python implementation computes the same outputs with different code. Both run over a grid of 960 input combinations — balances from $0 to $250,000, APYs from 0% to 18.75%, all four compounding frequencies, deposits from $0 to $1,500, and terms from 1 month to 30 years.
  4. Consistency. The month-by-month schedule must sum to the total, and the savings-goal solver must return the first month the goal is reached.

The engine is published at /assets/calc.js for anyone to read.

What the calculators do not do

  • They do not know current bank rates. Enter the APY your bank shows you.
  • They do not model rate changes, fees, minimum-balance tiers or promotional rates that expire.
  • They do not give tax advice. The tax estimate is a marginal-rate approximation.
  • They do not recommend any bank or product.
