High-yield savings accounts: pros, cons and risks

What you get, what you give up, and the risks that are real versus the ones that are not.

Pros: a much higher rate than a traditional savings account, FDIC insurance to $250,000, and access to your money within a few days with no penalty.

Cons: the rate is variable, interest is taxed as ordinary income, inflation can erase most of the real return, and transfers out are slower than at your main bank.

Advantages

  • Rate. The national average savings rate is 0.37% (September 21, 2026); high-yield accounts pay many times that.
  • Safety. A deposit at an FDIC-insured bank is covered to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. No market risk: the balance cannot fall.
  • Access. Unlike a CD, there is no penalty for taking money out.
  • Simplicity. Usually no minimum and no monthly fee.

Disadvantages

  • Variable rate. The bank can cut it at any time, and usually does when the Fed cuts. A CD locks a rate; a HYSA does not.
  • Tax. Interest is taxed as ordinary income each year, even if you leave it in the account.
  • Inflation. After tax, the real return can be close to zero (see below).
  • Slower access. Transfers to your checking account usually take one to three business days; most HYSAs have no debit card or branch.
  • Possible withdrawal limits. Some banks still cap withdrawals per month. What the rules are.
  • Teaser and tiered rates. A headline APY may be a promotion, or only apply to part of the balance.

The disadvantage in numbers

At 4.00% APY, a 24% federal bracket leaves 3.04% after tax. With 3% inflation, the real, after-tax return is about 0.04% a year. A HYSA protects your money from falling in value; it is not built to grow it. Try your own figures in the calculator's tax and inflation fields.

High-yield savings account risks, honestly

  • The bank failing — covered by FDIC insurance up to the limit. Check the bank on the FDIC's BankFind tool.
  • Balances above $250,000 at one bank in one ownership category are not covered. Split across banks or ownership categories.
  • Fintech apps. If an app holds your money at a partner bank, FDIC insurance covers the partner bank failing, not the app company failing. Know which bank actually holds the deposit.
  • Opportunity cost. Money you will not need for many years has historically done better invested — see HYSA vs investing.

Questions

What are the disadvantages of a high-yield savings account?

A variable rate that can be cut at any time, interest taxed as ordinary income, a real return that inflation can erase, and transfers that take one to three business days.

Is there any risk with a high-yield savings account?

Very little to the balance itself if the bank is FDIC-insured and you stay under $250,000 per bank per ownership category. The real risks are the rate falling and inflation.

Is a high-yield savings account worth it?

For cash you need to keep safe and reachable, usually yes: the national average savings account pays 0.37%, so moving an emergency fund to a high-yield account typically multiplies the interest many times over.
